Chapter 2 The binomial distribution

Let \(X\) be the number of successes in \(n\) tries. \(X\) follows a binomial distribution when four conditions hold:

  1. The number of tries \(n\) is fixed before the experiment starts.
  2. Each try has two outcomes, called success and failure.
  3. The chance of success \(p\) is the same on every try.
  4. The tries are independent: one try carries no information about the next.

Running example. A clinic tests 4 patients for flu. Each patient tests positive with probability 0.25, independently of the other patients. \(X\) is the number of positive tests.

2.1 The formula

The probability of exactly \(k\) successes is \[ P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!}. \] The term \(\binom{n}{k}\) counts the ways to choose which \(k\) tries are successes; the remaining term is the probability of one such arrangement. The mean is \(E(X) = np\) and the variance is \(\text{Var}(X) = np(1 - p)\). One try has mean \(p\) and variance \(p(1 - p)\), and the binomial adds \(n\) independent tries.

For the clinic, \(E(X) = 4 \times 0.25 = 1\) and \(\text{Var}(X) = 0.75\). Exactly one positive test can happen in \(\binom{4}{1} = 4\) ways, so \(P(X = 1) = 4 \times 0.25 \times 0.75^3 \approx 0.42\).

2.2 Adding up: the cumulative distribution

The cumulative distribution function adds the probabilities from 0 to \(x\): \(P(X \le 1) = 0.316 + 0.422 = 0.738\). For a count, “less than 2” and “at most 1” are the same event, and \(P(X \ge 2) = 1 - P(X \le 1) = 0.262\).

n <- 4
p <- 0.25
dbinom(0:n, size = n, prob = p)   # P(X = k) for k = 0, ..., 4
choose(4, 1)                      # ways to get exactly one positive
pbinom(1, size = n, prob = p)     # P(X <= 1)
1 - pbinom(1, size = n, prob = p) # P(X >= 2)
c(mean = n * p, variance = n * p * (1 - p))

Check your understanding. The clinic now tests 5 patients, and each tests positive with probability 0.2. The mean is 1, the variance is 0.8, \(P(X = 0) = 0.8^5 \approx 0.33\), and \(P(X \le 1) \approx 0.74\).

2.3 Flipping the question

So far, \(n\) and \(p\) were known and the question was about \(X\). Public health often runs the other way: a count is observed, and the question is how large the whole population is. The rest of the chapter asks whether the binomial can count a population that nobody can count directly.
